# Solenoids – Linear Motion from Coils

In this lesson, you'll learn how solenoids convert electrical energy into linear motion, perfect for locks, valves, and mechanical actuators.

<LessonChapter title="What You'll Learn" id="what-youll-learn">

- Solenoid structure and operation
- How the plunger moves when energized
- Why flyback diodes are essential
- Practical applications of solenoids

<InfoBox type="info">
  A solenoid is an electromagnetic coil that creates linear motion. When energized, it pulls or pushes a metal plunger to actuate mechanical systems.
</InfoBox>

</LessonChapter>

  A solenoid has a **wire coil** wrapped around a tube, a **metal plunger** that slides inside, and a **spring** that returns the plunger when power is removed.
</GifStep>

### Main Components

1. **Coil**: Wire wound around a tube
2. **Plunger**: Metal rod that moves in/out
3. **Spring**: Returns plunger when de-energized
4. **Frame**: Magnetic housing

### Types of Solenoids

- **Pull-type**: Plunger pulls in when energized
- **Push-type**: Plunger pushes out when energized
- **Latching**: Stays in position without power

>
  When current flows, the magnetic field pulls the plunger into the coil. This mechanical movement can push levers, open valves, or engage locks.
</GifStep>

### Energizing the Coil

When current flows through the coil:
1. A magnetic field is created
2. The field attracts the ferromagnetic plunger
3. Plunger moves against the spring
4. Mechanical work is done (push lever, open valve, etc.)

### De-energizing

When current stops:
1. Magnetic field collapses
2. Spring pushes plunger back
3. Returns to rest position

<InfoBox type="tip">
  Solenoids are used in door locks, pinball machines, car door latches, sprinkler valves, and fuel injectors!
</InfoBox>

</LessonChapter>

<LessonChapter title="Flyback Diode Protection" id="flyback-protection">

<GifStep
  stepNumber={3}
  title="Add the Flyback Diode"
  gifUrl="https://placehold.co/600x400/2563eb/white?text=Flyback+Diode+GIF"
  alt="Installing flyback diode"
>
  Connect a diode in **reverse** across the solenoid: cathode to positive, anode to negative. This provides a safe path for current when the solenoid turns off.
</GifStep>

### Why It's Critical

Solenoids are inductive loads (coils). When you turn them off:
1. Magnetic field collapses rapidly
2. Large voltage spike is generated (can be 100V+!)
3. This spike can destroy transistors and microcontrollers

### Adding Protection

Connect a flyback diode in **reverse** across the solenoid:
- Cathode to positive terminal
- Anode to negative terminal

When the spike occurs, the diode conducts and safely dissipates the energy.

<InfoBox type="warning">
  Never switch a solenoid without a flyback diode! The voltage spike will eventually destroy your circuit.
</InfoBox>
